**What the current search does about opponents**

At a decision during our turn, the solver evaluates stopping by banking the runners, advancing the active player, and asking the network for each seat's eventual win probability. Continuing integrates every future dice outcome and optimal in-turn choice until the turn ends. All strategic effects after that boundary are represented by the network's prediction. The features include opponents' saved progress, ownership, turn order, and rules. There is no missing opponent-state input apparent in this representation.

Consequently, the opponent is represented, but its future play is approximated. If a stop board and a risky-continuation board have different prediction errors, their ordering can be wrong even though the within-turn probability calculation is exact. In two-player play, a perfect continuation value would make the complete current-turn solve sufficient for optimal choices. “Only one turn” therefore does not impose a fundamental strength ceiling independent of evaluator quality. In multiplayer, specify the continuation policy profile; maximizing each mover's own win component is not equivalent to treating the other seats as a coalition minimizing ours.

The old MCTS's decisions changing with more simulations can reflect reduced Monte Carlo noise, additional depth into opponents' turns, exploration of neglected alternatives, or unstable estimates near a genuine tie. A change alone does not tell us which decision was better. The useful follow-up is to measure the two actions' eventual outcomes from the same position under a fixed continuation policy, then repeat with a stronger or different policy.

The current k=4 result is particularly weak evidence against deeper search. It refines the bust board and three stop boards selected under the initial policy, with the optional common offset. It does not evaluate all important competing plans equally, and it can overlook alternatives initially rejected by the network. In a four-player game, refining one next turn still omits the other two opponents' turns before we move again. A full round is a distinct horizon worth testing on selected positions.

**Measurements performed for this review**

I generated 32 ordinary self-play games with the current two-player checkpoint, producing 331 end-of-turn rows, and uniformly sampled 120 rows without replacement using seed 260926. Related boards are not independent statistical trials; this is a small diagnostic sample, not a strength estimate.

- **Immediate-threat bound:** for each sampled board, I reused the exact turn solver with continuation payoff zero for the mover at every nonwinning leaf and payoff one at a winning terminal. Its root is the maximum probability the mover can win before this turn ends. That is a lower bound on its achievable eventual win probability. The network violated this bound on none of the 120 boards, even before a one-point tolerance. This gives no evidence for the simplest immediate-threat failure in this sample. It does not test calibration above the bound, accuracy on counterfactual leaves, later opponent turns, or stop/roll ordering.
- **Column-reflection consistency:** reverse columns 2↔12, 3↔11, and so on, in every seat's progress and ownership features. The dice transformation d↦7−d preserves probabilities and maps sums c↦14−c; heights and blocking rules are symmetric. Best-play values should therefore agree. The checkpoint's mover probabilities differed by a mean absolute 0.703 percentage points, a maximum 8.574 points, and over one point on 24/120 boards. The current training path has no reflection augmentation.

This symmetry discrepancy is useful evidence of unlearned known structure, but it does not itself establish an Elo or win-rate gain. A fixed asymmetric policy can also have asymmetric policy values; the symmetry assertion is about the intended best-play game value. Test augmentation and optional mirrored prediction averaging in the arena. Random reflection at training time preserves seat targets and is inexpensive; averaging both orientations at inference doubles network evaluation work and needs its own speed/strength comparison. Do not permute arbitrary opponent seats in three- or four-player games: their turn order matters.

**The most informative next benchmark**

Build a frozen collection of actual legal stop/roll decision states, not only end-of-turn boards. Use several source policies and keep source-game IDs. Begin with the current two-player specialist so model quality in untrained variants does not confound the first diagnosis. Then apply the same benchmark to the target three-player variant once a suitable net exists.

Maintain separate strata: ordinary randomly sampled decisions; decisions with a small predicted stop/roll margin; imminent opponent threats; contested columns/blocking; and positions where different checkpoints or search budgets disagree. Report the strata separately, or weight them by their occurrence rates when estimating ordinary-play error. Oversampling difficult cases without weighting should not be presented as overall error.

For every position:

1. Record the solver's stop value, roll value, margin, and chosen action.
2. Force **stop now** in one branch and **roll once now** in the other. In the roll branch, future decisions in this turn remain available; do not accidentally evaluate “never stop again.”
3. Continue both branches to terminal with a frozen, identified controller for every seat. Run paired dice streams with correct marginal distributions. A seed shared by both branches is a basic coupling, but extra rolls can misalign it; a keyed stream by replicate, seat, turn occurrence, and roll index can improve pairing. Check empirical variance rather than assuming the coupling helps.
4. Estimate the difference in the original mover's terminal win probability. Report confidence on the difference, not overlapping separate error bars. Use fixed confirmation batches or valid sequential inference if allocating extra rollouts adaptively.
5. Report the proportion of materially wrong decisions and the estimated win-probability loss from those decisions. Use independent confirmation samples to avoid choosing and grading the best action on the same noisy outcomes.
6. Repeat a subset with one-full-round truncated lookahead, longer rollouts, and alternative opponent styles. Agreement that strengthens with horizon is evidence of an evaluator boundary problem. Differences that disappear with more trials are evidence that the apparent flips were sampling noise or negligible margins.

These rollouts estimate value under the named continuation policy, not perfect-play ground truth. Even so, they directly test whether the network's action comparison agrees with actual outcomes under its own controller. If deeper evaluation reliably repairs decisions and wins head-to-head at an acceptable time budget, then add that evaluation selectively. No reconstruction of the old AlphaZero pipeline is required: the exact turn solver can serve as the rollout controller, or as the within-turn component of a search over turn boundaries.

**Experiments that distinguish the likely bottlenecks**

| Experiment | What a positive result would mean | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Paired action rollouts reverse decisions with independently confirmed value gains | Continuation evaluation/search leaves useful strength on the table | Selective rollout search, then distill stronger values |
| Random reflection improves strength or action regret at fixed training budget | Representation/sample efficiency was limiting | Keep augmentation; assess inference averaging separately |
| Larger nets beat smaller ones on independent, lower-noise labels with enough data | Capacity matters on the relevant target | Increase size or add a specialized head |
| More data or better labels helps while more epochs does not | The frozen-data capacity test hit a data/target limit | Generate broader data or stronger targets |
| A target-variant specialist beats the generalist at matched exposure | Shared training causes interference or inadequate per-variant capacity | Adjust sampling or use per-player-count/variant heads |
| Fresh/reanalyzed targets beat replayed targets at comparable cost | Target lag is limiting learning | Shorter replay or selective reanalysis |
| All tested improvements fit within a predeclared small margin on held-out strength and action tests | Local saturation of the tested model/data/compute setup | Spend resources on other variants or a different teacher |

For the capacity experiment, separate optimization from generalization: overfit a small low-noise dataset, then plot held-out performance across both dataset size and network size, with multiple seeds. Tune optimization enough that “same epochs” does not merely handicap one model. CE minus target entropy is useful, but with stochastic labels it includes irreducible variation in labels conditional on the board; it is not all removable model error. Evaluate decision loss as well as average probability error.

An additional distribution issue deserves explicit testing: training collects the one board actually reached after a turn, while the solver evaluates many thousands of counterfactual stopping boards. Self-play labels do not automatically cover those rejected alternatives. Sample a modest quota of stop/bust alternatives near consequential decisions, label them with independent rollouts or a stronger search, and mix them with ordinary game data. Merely asking the same network to relabel its own selected leaves can preserve its existing blind spots.

**Backgammon lessons worth applying**

1. **Learned values and rollout search are complementary.** Tesauro and Galperin evaluated actions with full or truncated rollouts driven by a fixed base player, and used a separate position suite to measure decision error. This is directly relevant to the reported stop/roll flips: measure whether extra computation improves the chosen action. Their numerical gains do not predict gains in Can't Stop, but the experimental design transfers. [Original paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/1996/file/996009f2374006606f4c0b0fda878af1-Paper.pdf).

2. **Exact TD targets do not imply that additional search has stopped helping.** PureTD's own Table 6 reports lower offline error with increased search depth; those are backgammon XG-analysis results, not direct Can't Stop evidence. Its sampled-to-exact schedule is a sensible ablation to copy, but it does not validate this plan's replay window, passes, or plateau trigger. [PureTD, sections 3.2 and 4.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.15146v1).

3. **Reduce dice variance in evaluation, as well as in training.** GNU Backgammon documents variance-reduced rollouts and stratified dice. The applicable idea is a zero-mean control variate: subtract an estimate of how much a sampled roll changed the outcome relative to the exact average over rolls. That can make action comparisons more precise without requiring the value estimator itself to be perfect. [GNU Backgammon manual](https://www.gnu.org/software/gnubg/manual/gnubg.html).

   For a Can't Stop implementation, choose a fixed reference function h_i(s,d) in one absolute seat's coordinates and compute m_i(s)=Σ_d p(d)h_i(s,d). The correction h_i(s,d_actual)−m_i(s) has conditional expectation zero. Subtracting accumulated corrections from the terminal winner indicator preserves the mean, provided dice probabilities, perspectives, and conditioning are correct. The exact turn tables offer a natural source of these values. If a branch has been stratified/forced, use its correct conditional distribution rather than pretending it is an ordinary draw. Validate corrected means against ordinary rollouts and exact tiny cases before relying on the variance estimates.

   Corrected individual outcomes may lie outside [0,1]. Do not clip them when estimating an unbiased mean, and do not feed them directly to the current probability-target cross-entropy. This proposal is initially for diagnostics; converting noisy estimates into training targets is a separate choice. Common random numbers, stratification, and control variates solve related but different variance problems. None corrects a poor rollout policy by itself.

4. **Use exact or strongly labeled tactical subsets.** GNU Backgammon also uses bearoff databases. The analogous opportunity is a small solved late-game suite, when computationally feasible, plus independently labeled threat/column-race positions. Cross-turn busts can cycle back to the same saved board, so a purported exact endgame solver must handle those cycles with suitable fixed-point/policy-evaluation methods rather than assume the whole game is the acyclic within-turn table. [GNU Backgammon features](https://www.gnu.org/s/gnubg/).

5. **Test representation improvements where they matter.** Symmetry augmentation is the first candidate here because the game symmetry is exact and the probe found visible discrepancies. After that, a next-turn-win auxiliary target, distance-to-claim features, or opponent-race features are reasonable ablations. The encoder's assertion that the turn solver makes all probability features redundant is too broad: it calculates the mover's turn, while the network must predict future players' races and blocking effects. Such features may improve learning efficiency even when they add no new state information. Avoid introducing several at once; verify any benefit with held-out decision tests and the arena.

**Suggested order of work**

Keep the existing two-player checkpoint as the diagnostic baseline. First add the forced-action benchmark, experiment metadata, and final confirmation evaluation. Test reflection augmentation against a matched continuation of the same checkpoint. In parallel with that experimental sequence—not a requirement for parallel agents—implement the generalist baseline's row balancing and per-variant logs. Run a modest generalist pilot and a target-variant specialist baseline. Then choose between stronger labels/search, more data, or more capacity based on which tests reveal a gap. Reserve automatic exact-target staging and large architecture sweeps for controlled comparisons rather than making every promising idea part of one run.

I would retain the exact turn solver. The strongest unresolved question is how accurately its end-of-turn values rank the stop and continuation alternatives, especially across an entire opponent round.
